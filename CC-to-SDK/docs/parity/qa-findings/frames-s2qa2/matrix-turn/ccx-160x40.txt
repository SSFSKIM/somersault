Mill secured an English patent for a device to impress letters, though nothing survives of it. Practical designs
followed in the nineteenth century, including Pellegrino Turri's machine built around 1808 for a blind countess. The
decisive breakthrough came in 1868, when Christopher Latham Sholes, Carlos Glidden, and Samuel Soulé patented a model in
 Milwaukee; Remington began manufacturing it in 1873, introducing the QWERTY layout that persists today. Later
refinements brought visible typing, shift keys for lowercase, and portable models that carried journalists and novelists
 through two world wars. Electric machines, notably IBM's Selectric of 1961 with its rotating typeball, dominated
offices until word processors and personal computers displaced them in the 1980s.
