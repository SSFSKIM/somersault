⏺ The History of the Terminal Emulator

  The terminal emulator's story begins not with software, but with hardware. In the 1960s and 70s, computing relied on
  physical teletypewriters and video display terminals—devices like the Teletype Model 33 and the DEC VT100—which served
  as the primary interface between humans and mainframe or minicomputer systems. These terminals were literally
  hardwired, converting keystrokes into electrical signals sent to a distant computer and rendering returned text on
  paper or screen.

  The VT100, released by Digital Equipment Corporation in 1978, became especially influential. Its escape sequence
  protocol for cursor movement, text formatting, and screen control became a de facto standard, and its legacy persists
  today: countless modern terminal emulators still describe themselves as "VT100-compatible."

  As personal computers emerged in the late 1970s and 1980s, the physical terminal began to disappear, but the
  interaction model it established did not. Software began emulating these hardware terminals, allowing a PC to connect
  to remote systems as if it were a VT100 or similar device. Programs like Kermit and various Unix utilities extended
  this emulation to handle file transfers and diverse protocols.

  The rise of Unix and later Linux cemented the terminal emulator as a persistent software layer. Programs like xterm,
  developed in the 1980s for the X Window System, allowed graphical operating systems to host terminal sessions as
  windows alongside other applications, decoupling terminal emulation entirely from physical hardware.

  From the 1990s onward, terminal emulators diversified with the growth of open-source and consumer operating systems:
  rxvt, Terminal.app, GNOME Terminal, PuTTY, and later Windows Terminal each catered to different platforms and
  workflows.

  Today's terminal emulators—including iTerm2, Alacritty, Kitty, and WezTerm—prioritize GPU acceleration, Unicode
  support, and extensibility, while still faithfully interpreting decades-old escape sequences, a remarkable testament
  to the durability of a standard born from obsolete hardware.
