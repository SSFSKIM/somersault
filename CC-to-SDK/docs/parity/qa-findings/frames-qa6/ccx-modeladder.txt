❯ Write a 300-word essay about the history of the terminal emulator. No tools.
⏺ The terminal emulator's history begins with hardware it no longer resembles. In the 1960s, computing terminals were
physical machines: the Teletype Model 33, an electromechanical printer that typed onto paper rolls, connected users to
distant mainframes at ten characters per second. Its constraints still echo in modern systems — the "tty" device name is
 short for teletype, and the carriage return and line feed characters once described real mechanical motions.

  Glass replaced paper in the early 1970s. Video display terminals like the DEC VT05, and later the enormously
influential VT100 of 1978, drew characters on a cathode ray tube. The VT100 implemented an emerging standard for escape
sequences — short bursts of control characters that moved the cursor, cleared regions of the screen, or changed text
attributes. That specification, eventually ratified as ANSI X3.64 and ECMA-48, became the lingua franca of text
interfaces and remains the reason a program written in 1980 can still color its output in a window today.

  Emulation proper arrived when terminals stopped being separate machines. As workstations and personal computers gained
 their own processors and displays, software began imitating the VT100's behavior, letting a general-purpose computer
masquerade as dedicated hardware. The MIT X Window System brought xterm in 1984, a program that drew a terminal inside a
 graphical window — a simulation of a device many users had never physically touched. Successors multiplied: rxvt, GNOME
 Terminal, Konsole, PuTTY on Windows, Terminal.app on the Mac.

  Rather than fading, the form has proven remarkably durable. Developers still spend their days inside emulators, and
recent projects — GPU-accelerated ones like Alacritty and Kitty, and full-featured entrants like Ghostty and WezTerm —
compete on rendering speed and typography. The paper rolls are gone, but their protocol survived, a layer of 1970s
convention faithfully reproduced by software fast enough to render it thousands of times per second.
